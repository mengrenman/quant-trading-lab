# Project Handoff: Limit Order Book (LOB) Simulation

## 1. Executive Summary

This project is an interactive, educational **Limit Order Book (LOB) Simulation & Visualization Platform** built in Python and modern web technologies. It is based on the foundational research paper:

> **Gould, M.D., Porter, M.A., Williams, S., McDonald, M., Fenn, D.J. and Howison, S.D.** (2013).  
> *Limit order books.* Quantitative Finance, 13(11), 1709–1742.  
> [doi:10.1080/14697688.2013.803148](https://doi.org/10.1080/14697688.2013.803148) | [arXiv:1012.0349](https://arxiv.org/abs/1012.0349)

The platform pairs a pure-Python matching engine and stochastic market simulation models with thirteen teaching notebooks and a real-time web dashboard (FastAPI, Server-Sent Events, HTMX, Tailwind CSS, and TradingView Lightweight Charts). It lets researchers and students study market microstructure, order queues, emergent bid-ask spreads, and stylized facts (heavy tails, volatility clustering, price impact) through interactive experimentation.

The project is under git; Section 6 lists the changes since the baseline snapshot of 2026-11-01.

### Key Modules & Files

- **`lob/book.py`**:
  - `OrderBook`: Dual-sided order book with tick size and lot size validation.
  - Price levels live in `SortedDict`s (bid keys negated so iteration runs best-first); each level is a FIFO `deque`.
  - O(1) order lookup, depth-at-price queries and uniform random order sampling. Canceling removes the order from its level's queue, which is linear in that queue's length.
  - `validate(order)` checks tick size, lot size and order-ID uniqueness. Both engines call it *before* matching, so a rejected order never leaves the book half-changed.
  - `orders_at(price, side)` returns a level's queue in priority order.
  - Iceberg orders (`display_size`): while an order rests, `size` is its visible part, which is all that depths, depth profiles, snapshots and matching see, and `hidden` is the reserve (`hidden_depth_at_price` sums reserves at a price). An arriving iceberg trades its full size and is split when it comes to rest.
- **`lob/matching.py`**:
  - `PriceTimeEngine`: Price-time priority (FIFO within each price level).
  - `ProRataEngine`: Allocates proportionally to resting order sizes in whole lots, rounding down and handing leftover lots out in time priority, so fills and remainders stay multiples of the lot size.
  - Engines reduce resting orders' `size` in place as they fill.
  - When an iceberg's peak is filled, the next comes from its reserve and joins the back of its price level (losing time priority), so an incoming order can fill more than the visible depth; pro-rata shares follow visible sizes and the level is allocated again after refills. Ordinary orders behave exactly as before (every model's output hashes identically).
- **`lob/models/`** (all models continue from the caller's clock when it is ahead of their own):
  - `zero_intelligence.SmithFarmerModel`: Zero-intelligence model of Daniels et al. (2003) / Smith et al. (2003), Gould et al. Section 5.2.4. Limit orders land uniformly on the `price_range` ticks next to the *opposite* best quote, so they fall inside the spread with probability proportional to its width; market orders and per-order cancellations are Poisson. `_sample_side` / `_sample_size` hooks let ablations change signs and sizes before an order is priced.
  - `hawkes.HawkesZIModel`: The ZI model with market orders arriving as a self-exciting (Hawkes) process. The baseline rate is scaled to `mu * (1 - branching)` so the long-run arrival rate stays `mu`; every arrival excites, even one that finds the opposite side empty. Simulated exactly by thinning.
  - `mike_farmer.MikeFarmerModel`: Simplified Mike & Farmer (2008): power-law relative prices measured from the mid-price (always at least one tick), long-memory order signs, log-normal sizes, constant-rate cancellation.
  - `mike_farmer.MikeFarmer2008Model`: Mike and Farmer's structure: prices set relative to the same-side best quote with Student's t offsets (orders can land inside the spread or cross it and execute as effective market orders; no separate market order process), long-memory signs, and state-dependent cancellation simulated by thinning. Parameter values are illustrative, not their LSE calibration.
  - `long_memory.LongMemorySigns`: A continuous stream of long-memory signs (signs of FARIMA(0, H − 1/2, 0) noise, applied by overlap-save FFT convolution) whose memory never resets.
  - `strategic.ParlourModel`: A hand-tuned rule inspired by Parlour (1998): the probability of a market order rises with a narrow spread and a long queue at the best quotes. It is not a game-theoretic equilibrium model.
- **`lob/simulation.py`**: `Simulation.run()` returns the event log as a DataFrame (stored internally in typed arrays: a 400k-event run takes about 190 MB); `Simulation.trades` has one row per fill; `Simulation.snapshots` holds book snapshots. The log's `bid_size` and `ask_size` are the visible depth at the best quotes.
- **`lob/calibration.py`**: maximum-likelihood estimates of every `MikeFarmer2008Model` parameter from an event log that starts from an empty book (arrival rate; rounded Student's t offsets; the signs' Hurst exponent; the state-dependent cancellation rate and imbalance offset, from replaying the log through a book). Notebook 08 checks them by recovering known parameters.
- **`lob/lobster.py`**: LOBSTER (Nasdaq) data: `read_lobster`/`save_lobster` for the message and order-book files, `lobster_orders` (messages to orders: executions at one time against one side are one aggressive order, a new order of its side at the same time is its resting part), and `calibrate_lobster`, the same estimates allowing for what such data hold back. An order that executes in full leaves no limit price, only a bound (its worst fill), so the price fit treats its offset as censored (`fit_rounded_t_censored`, exact for the model); the cancellation replay follows only orders the file shows placed, with quotes from the order-book file, after a burn-in (`last_unknown` says how long one needs). `simulated_lobster` writes the model's runs in LOBSTER's format, hiding what Nasdaq's feed hides.
- **`lob/strategies.py`**: the quantitative strategies the dashboard runs for the user (see 3M). Each `decide`s every `interval` of simulation time from a `View` (the best quotes, the lots at them, its own position) and answers with a `Plan`: quotes to rest (`MarketMaking`; `AvellanedaStoikov`, around a reservation price with a volatility estimated online; `PennyJumping`, a tick ahead of the best quotes), or a position to hold (`Momentum`: fast against slow exponential averages of the mid; `MeanReversion`: z-score of the mid over a window, with running sums; `Breakout`: a Donchian channel; `QueueImbalance`: the best queues' imbalance, after Gould & Bonart 2016, flat when it tips back; `OrderFlowImbalance`: Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov's OFI between decisions over a window, in average queue lengths; `RandomTrader`: a benchmark with its own xorshift64* generator, so it repeats exactly; `QuoteJoiner`: the null for making markets, at the best bid and ask and nothing else). A strategy keeps only its signal's state (`state`/`restore`, for recordings); its runner keeps the account. One that `keeps_queue` leaves a resting quote alone while it is at the price wanted.
- **`lob/pairs.py`**: two order books whose prices move together (notebooks 10 and 11). `FairValues`: a common random walk and a part of each book's own, which order flow moves (impact) and which, with a half-life, decays back, so the gap mean-reverts (`gap_sd`); without one the gap wanders. Two options, off by default: `beta_sd` makes beta drift (B moves beta_t times the factor's moves, added up in `b_common`; `gap_of` is the gap of two prices in these terms), and `stress_share` puts the common factor in a stressed regime, more volatile and perhaps drifting, the calm regime drifting the other way. `AnchoredZIModel`: zero-intelligence order flow around a book's fair value, so the book reprices as it moves. `Arbitrageur`: buys the cheap book and sells the dear one past a threshold, through its impact the only reversion when there is no half-life. Three traders, sharing `decide` (which trades on `observe`, the signal, and `want`, the rule), `learn` (past mids, with no trades), `warming`, `deviation` and `width` (its levels on the gap) and `signals`: `PairsTrader`, the gap's z-score over a window at a fixed hedge (`record` keeps each decision, for an exact daily record); `KalmanTrader`, on `KalmanHedge`, whose slope is the hedge and whose error, over its usual size, is the z-score; `CopulaTrader`, on `lob/copulas.py`, the rule of Rad et al. (2016). `PairMarket`: both books on one clock, `submit` for outside orders, `mark` for valuing positions while a side is empty, `run` sampling everything.
- **`lob/copulas.py`**: bivariate copulas for pairs trading: Gaussian, Student t, Clayton, Gumbel, Frank, `Rotated` (the survival copula of another) and `Independence`, each with `h` (the conditional probability), `cdf`, `logpdf`, `tail_dependence` and `sample`. `fit_copula` fits each family by canonical maximum likelihood (from the value Kendall's tau implies; the t's nu on a grid) and takes the lowest AIC. `pseudo_observations` and `Margin` rank returns, ties sharing their mean rank, as prices on half ticks tie.
- **`lob/statarb.py`**: many books with several common factors, and traders of many relations (notebook 12, see 3R). `FactorValues`: common factors through a table of loadings, and each book's own part, on a grid of time from a random stream of their own. `BookMarket` runs the books on one clock. `RelationTrader` trades the z-score of each of a set of portfolios, fixed or chosen by a function of past mids (`johansen_relations`, `best_baskets`, `pairs_by_distance`, `pairs_by_pvalue`); `ResidualTrader` is stat-arb on PCA residuals (Avellaneda and Lee 2010); `CopulaPortfolio` runs `lob.pairs.CopulaTrader` on each of many pairs. `score_portfolio` measures a trader over a span alone; `sector_loadings` and `mixed_loadings` make the two kinds of market. `BookMarket.flow` and `volume` keep, a book each, the lots of the models' own orders that traded on arrival (signed, and not), and `BookTrader.observe` shows a trader the market just before it decides (3S.3). Every `BookTrader` has a `rate` and a `band` (1 and 0 by default): with a rate below 1 its aim goes only that share of the way to its target at a decision, and with a band above 0 it does not trade within that many lots of its aim (`BookTrader._toward`, 3S.7).
- **`lob/crosssection.py`**: cross-sectional long/short traders over the same books (notebook 13, see 3S). A `CrossSectionTrader` scores every book, takes the scores less their projection on the ones and the books' exposures to estimated factors (`neutralize`, `estimate_factors`: the PCA of `ResidualTrader`), and holds lots in proportion, rounded. `ReversalTrader` buys the books that fell, `MomentumTrader` those that rose (over a look back that may end some decisions ago), `LeadLagTrader` those that a ridge regression on the other books' last moves says will rise, and `FlowTrader` those with the buying in their trades or their queues. `contrarian_decomposition` splits a contrarian portfolio's profit into the books' own and cross autocovariances and the dispersion of their means (Lo and MacKinlay 1990). With a rate below 1 or a band above 0 a trader goes toward its target at every decision, and not only at a rebalance (3S.7).
- **`lob/sparse_baskets.py`**: baskets chosen by Box and Tiao's predictability and not by Johansen's test (notebook 13's section 6, see 3S.8). `fit_var1` fits a VAR(1) to the books' levels, `predictability` is the share of a portfolio's variance that the lagged levels explain, `box_tiao` the portfolios of some books by predictability (a generalized eigenproblem, least first), `greedy_basket` grows a set of books one at a time (d'Aspremont 2011) and `sparse_baskets` does that from every pair, ranks the distinct sets, and gives them in `best_baskets`' shape, so a `RelationTrader` takes it as its `choose`. It tests nothing, is scale-free and searches greedily (3S.8).
- **`lob/impact.py`**: a metaorder's impact against a twin run from the same book, model and random state that sends nothing (common random numbers): `warmed_market`, `paired_metaorders` (notebook 09).
- **`lob/metrics.py`**, **`lob/analysis/`**: returns (`event_time_returns`), per-order VWAP (`trades_by_order`), virtual price impact on book snapshots, ACF with white-noise bands, rank-based volatility ACF, Hurst (R/S), Hill and power-law estimators, time-averaged depth profiles, order flow imbalance (`order_flow_imbalance`, Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov 2014) with `ofi_regression`, and plotting helpers.
- **`app/sessions.py`**: `SessionRegistry` holds one `LOBManager` per browser session, found through a random ID in an HTTP-only, SameSite=Lax cookie (tabs of one browser share it). IDs are issued only by the server. Sessions without an open stream are evicted after 30 minutes idle, and the least recently seen one beyond 50 sessions; eviction stops the session's simulation.
- **`app/manager.py`**:
  - `LOBManager`: One session's book, engine, model, parameters, seed, background runner and manual orders.
  - One simulation clock (`sim_time`): model events advance it by the model's own event times; each manual action advances it by `MANUAL_ACTION_DT` (0.5).
  - Executed trades are stored as one-unit bars (`deque(maxlen=100_000)`); `get_candles()` rebuilds OHLCV bars, with buy and sell volume, for any interval.
  - `reset()` restarts the random generator from the session's seed, so the same seed and parameters replay a run exactly.
  - Every move of the clock goes through `_advance`, which starts an order-book heatmap column (`HeatColumn`) at each whole unit of time it reaches: the visible depth 30 ticks either side of the mid just before the event that reached it (bids positive, asks negative), the user's iceberg reserves, and the lots traded during the unit. The most recent 600 are kept; none are taken during a collection, after which the heatmap starts over (as after a reset, with a new `heat_version`).
  - The user's fills, as maker or taker (a fill against their own order counts on both sides), are kept per unit of time (`OwnBar`); `own_trade_marks` aggregates them to the chart's interval for its markers.
  - `move_orders` moves the user's orders at one price to another as one manual action (each canceled and entered again with what it had left and the same iceberg peak, in its old queue order); `cancel_all_orders` cancels theirs, optionally on one side or at one price. `cancel_order_by_id` cancels only the user's own orders.
  - `Account` books a trader's fills by average cost (realized P&L on reducing a position, the rest marked to the mid) and scores each fill against the mid before it (edge) and 100 events later (markout, `MARKOUT_EVENTS`; in events so that models with different event rates compare like with like). The user has one (`account`), and each of their strategies another.
  - `RunningStrategy` and `start_strategy`/`stop_strategy`/`resume_strategy`/`remove_strategy`: the user's strategies (see 3M), at most `MAX_STRATEGIES`. After each model event, `_run_strategy` asks each one that is due for its plan, from `_view`: the book without the strategy's own orders (it knows which are its, so a market maker never quotes around its own quotes), and its position. Quotes are withdrawn and placed afresh; a target position is reached by a protected market order whose rest is canceled (aggressive), or by one order at the best quote, left alone while it is still the one wanted so it keeps its place in the queue (passive). Like the user's orders, theirs (`_strategy_orders`) are never canceled by the models; their fills go to their own accounts. `strategies_summary` is the leaderboard, with the user's row and one P&L sample per unit of time for each sparkline. Reset rebuilds each from its settings with a fresh account.
  - The regime and the nulls (see 3P): `_takes` and `_observe_trade` follow the spread just before each trade and the mid's change from one trade to the next; `_market_markouts` and `_flow` what the model's own resting and market orders, and the quote joiners, earn 100 events after they trade (weighted averages over the last 2,000 or so, which fit in a recording's starting state); `regime` reports them; at each markout, `_null_rate` gives the null a fill is held against (`Account.null_*`).
  - Fitting (see 3O): `training_tape` makes a training period from the session as it stands, with random numbers of its own; `fit_strategy` replays it once for each combination of the values given, the candidate trading from its start, and ranks them (`Fit`); `start_fitted` starts one, keeping its training record (`RunningStrategy.fit`).
  - Recording: `_process` records each order as it arrives (before matching changes it), `_cancel_resting` is the one path for the user's and strategies' cancellations (and records them), and engine, model and strategy changes are noted. `start_recording`/`stop_recording` keep at most `MAX_RECORDINGS`; Reset saves one in progress, and one stops by itself at `MAX_EVENTS`.
  - Playback: `start_playback` loads a recording's starting state (`_load_recording_start`, also `rewind_playback`), after which `step` takes orders and cancellations from the tape (`_step_playback`/`_apply`) instead of the model: each recorded order is entered afresh (ids mapped), as the user's or a replayed strategy's when it was. Replayed strategies (`replayed`) re-decide from the same view when they did (`_follow_decision`, so their signals and state follow along) but the tape carries their orders. The user and strategies started during a playback act as usual; a cancellation whose order is gone is skipped. `seek_playback` goes forward through the tape, or from its start to go back; `exit_playback` lets the selected model take over the book.
- **`app/live.py`**: `LiveMarket`, what the Pairs and Stat-arb views' markets have alike: their speed, run loop (`_run`, paced by the wall clock), `advance` for steps, and the streams to wake.
- **`app/pairs.py`**: the Pairs view (see 3Q.5). `TRADER_KINDS` says what each kind offers (settings, how to make one, how to describe it), and the form and endpoint are generated from it. A setting is a `Param` (a number in a range), a `Switch` or a `Choice` (named values, a drop-down: the Stat-arb view's order flow trader reads queues or trades). `PairsSession`, one per browser session (`LOBManager.pairs`, made when first needed and shut down with the session): a `PairMarket` from the settings (`MARKET_PARAMS`, applied with `configure`, which resets), its run loop (`_run`, paced by the wall clock), `advance` for steps, and up to `MAX_TRADERS` `PairsRunner`s (`start_trader`, optionally learning from the chart; `set_active`; `remove`). `_event` steps the market, notes each change of a trader's target (`marks`) and takes a sample at each whole unit of time (`_sample`: mids, fair values, both gaps, beta, and each trader's P&L, levels in the chart's terms, signal and hedge). `board`, `book_levels`, `gap_sds` and `context` are what the panels show; `history`, `samples_since` and `marks_since` feed the chart. For fitting (see 3Q.10) it says what a fit here tries (`fit_plan`, within `MAX_FIT_UNITS`) and how one candidate trades alone in a fresh market (`training_run`); the fit itself, `start_fitted` and the one-at-a-time pool are `app/training.py`'s.
- **`app/statarb.py`**: the Stat-arb view (see 3R.6, 3S.5 and 3S.8). `TRADER_KINDS` and `MARKET_PARAMS` say what it offers, as in `app/pairs.py`, and `CROSS_SECTIONAL` names the four kinds that score every book. ## 3. Web Dashboard Features

### A. Sessions and Live Updates
1. **One simulation per browser.** Each browser gets its own simulation; tabs of the same browser share it.
2. **One event stream per tab** carries the panels, the chart and the controls. ### C. Candlestick Chart & Volume Dual-Panel

3. **Stacked volume**: green is buy-initiated volume, red sell-initiated.

Bars pushed while its answer is on the way wait for it (`heldBars` in `chart.html`): after a reset or a rewind they can be older than what the series still holds, which Lightweight Charts refuses with an error, and those the answer has not got yet must not be lost. ### D. Depth of Market, Controls and Performance

### E. Order Entry
1. Limit and market orders. The Bid, Mid and Ask quick-price buttons follow the book (Mid rounds down for a buy, up for a sell).
2. Every order reports what happened under the button: resting as order #id, filled at what prices (with the average when it walked the book), what rests, or nothing filled. `/api/order/limit` and `/api/order/market` return that line as HTML (status 200); rejected input gets a message too.
3. The user's resting orders stay until they fill or the user cancels them. The models' cancellations stand for other traders withdrawing their own orders, so `LOBManager.step` skips one aimed at a manual order (counted as a step without an event). The models choose the order to cancel uniformly from the book, so this is exact thinning: their own orders keep their cancellation rate.
4. Manual market orders carry price protection, as on CME Globex: they walk the book at most N ticks (default 10, from 0 to 1,000, set in the form) past the best opposite price on arrival, and whatever is left rests there as the user's limit order, the paper's rule for an order that runs out of matches (Gould et al. p. 1712). Protection 0 fills only at the best price, like a market-to-limit order; with no opposite side nothing is placed. The result line says what was filled and what rests. The models' own market orders are unchanged: what they cannot fill is discarded.
5. Iceberg limit orders: an optional "show at most (lots)" field. The order book shows only the peak, marked "you 10 +90 hidden"; My Active Orders shows the reserve.
6. An order that trades is compared with the session VWAP from just before it: "Your average was 1.0% below the session VWAP of 101.00."
7. Fills of the user's resting orders are announced: a notice in each open tab ("Your buy order #583 filled 5 lots at 100, 3 left.") and the latest five under My Active Orders.
8. Split orders (TWAP): a market order can go out as N market orders, one every T units of simulation time. Each slice is protected; what it cannot fill rolls into the next. At the start the manager records what one market order for the whole size would have done (`sweep_estimate`); the summary compares the split's average with it and with the market's VWAP over the period. Slices wait while the simulation is paused; Stop ends a split.
9. **Cancel all** in My Active Orders cancels every resting order of the user's (`POST /api/order/cancel_all`, which can also take a side or a price) and leaves the models' alone.

### M. Strategies
1. A Strategies card across the bottom of the Trade view starts quantitative strategies of the user's, up to twelve at once, each trading with an account of its own as the simulation runs. Making markets: **market making** (a bid and an ask a set number of ticks from the mid, leaning against its inventory and never across the spread; at its inventory limit it quotes only the side that reduces it), **Avellaneda-Stoikov** (2008, stationary form: around a reservation price of the mid less position x risk aversion x variance x horizon, a spread of risk aversion x variance x horizon + (2/risk aversion) ln(1 + risk aversion/k), the variance per unit of time from the mid's changes over a window) and **penny jumping** (a tick ahead of the best bid and ask when the spread allows, joining them otherwise, not stepping ahead on the side that would add to its position, and keeping its orders while they are where it wants them). Trading on a signal: **momentum** (long while a fast exponential average of the mid is above a slow one by a threshold, short while below, flat once they cross), **mean reversion** (short when the mid is a number of standard deviations above its average over a window, long when as far below, flat back near it), **breakout** (long above the highest mid of its window, short below the lowest, flat back past the middle), **queue imbalance** (long once the best bid's queue outweighs the ask's by a threshold, short in the opposite case, flat when it tips back) and **order flow imbalance** (the OFI between decisions summed over a window, in average queue lengths at the best quotes; long beyond a threshold, short below minus it, flat when it tips past zero). And two benchmarks: **random**, which now and then takes a random position, and the **quote joiner**, the null for making markets, which rests at the best bid and ask and nothing else (3P). The signal strategies trade by crossing the spread, a protected market order, or by resting at the best quote. Their orders show by tag in both order-book views.
2. The leaderboard ranks them, and the user, by P&L: a sparkline of each one's P&L, what it holds, the lots it traded, its edge against the mid at each fill, the markout 100 events later, adverse selection (the difference), its signal now and the position it wants, and Stop (its orders withdrawn, what it holds kept and marked to the mid), Resume and Remove. The board is pushed with the panels, so it holds still while the pointer or the keyboard is on a row's buttons; what a click did shows in its row at once.
3. The market-making strategy replays the earlier quoting bot fill for fill (checked on three seeds). Checked over 8 seeds of 60,000 events in the ZI model: requoting every 15 events instead of 58 raised its adverse selection from 0.20 ± 0.05 to 0.40 ± 0.04 ticks a lot, on 262 instead of 472 lots (each new quote joins the back of its queue). An earlier comparison across models, which suggested that long-memory signs raise adverse selection, did not survive matching the requote interval in events.
4. The defaults came from runs of 20,000 to 30,000 events on three seeds in two or three models. Queue imbalance first went flat whenever the imbalance came back within its threshold and traded 10 lots: in 30,000 events of the ZI model it traded about 7,000 lots and lost about 8,800 ticks (the mean of three seeds). Waiting until the balance tips, with 5 lots, cut the loss about fivefold, there and in Mike & Farmer (2008). Its signal does predict the next move (its markout beat its edge by 0.4 to 0.9 ticks a lot), but by less than crossing the spread costs. In the simplified Mike & Farmer model, whose best queues hold about 900 lots (about 20 in the ZI and 2008 models), resting orders almost never fill.
5. On their defaults, in 20,000 events of each of three models (ZI, Mike & Farmer 2008, Parlour; three seeds each), Avellaneda-Stoikov and penny jumping made money in all nine runs, and breakout, order flow imbalance and the random trader lost in all nine: their fills cost about half the spread or more (edge -0.8 to -2.0 ticks a lot) and the price did not make it back.

### N. Recording and Playback
1. **Record**, next to Reset Book, keeps the session from that moment: the book as it stands, then every order and cancellation (the model's, the user's and the strategies'), until it is stopped, the book is reset, or it reaches 500,000 events. The status badge shows REC and the count; a session keeps three recordings (Analyze, Recordings), each downloadable as an `.npz` file and uploadable again.
2. **Play back** makes the session the recording's: its book, the user's orders and account, its strategies (replayed, following the tape), paused at the start. The tape takes the model's place, so Play, Pause, Step, the speed and the keyboard work as usual (a step is an order or a cancellation), and a playback bar shows where it is, with a timeline to drag; Reset rewinds. The engines are deterministic, so a playback reproduces the session exactly: the book order by order, every fill, the user's and every strategy's account and markouts, the clock (checked from the middle of a run with an iceberg, a market order, a split order, a move, Cancel all, strategies of every kind, one stopped and resumed and one removed, an engine toggle and a model change, from memory and through a file).
3. The user can trade, and start strategies, during a playback: they meet the recorded order flow, which does not react to them, so the market then goes its own way (cancellations of orders that are gone are skipped). Starting strategies on a playback before pressing Play runs them on the same market from the same start. **Exit playback** carries on live from there with the model selected (its state starts fresh, and the random generator restarts from the seed).
4. Recording adds about 12% to the time per event; a playback replays about 200,000 events a second, so seeking back (the tape again from its start) takes at most about 2.5 s. A 500,000-event file is about 5 MB. A range input rounds the time it gives (Chrome to 15 digits), so a seek counts events a hair later as reached.

### O. Fitting a Strategy over a Training Period
1. **Fit it over a training period**, in the Strategies card, tries the combinations of the values given for the chosen strategy's settings (each spec proposes some for its main ones; at most 8 values a setting, and combinations that contradict each other are left out) on one training period: all of them, or, when there are more than a fit tries (48, and fewer over a long period, to stay within 1,500,000 candidate events), a random sample of them drawn from the seed. That is a random search, which with many settings tends to do about as well as the whole grid (Bergstra and Bengio 2012); the card counts the combinations as settings are ticked, and the result says when it sampled. Ticking a fourth of mean reversion's settings used to be refused (81 combinations, more than 48); it now tries 48 of them. The candidates are ranked by **P&L** at the end, marked to the mid, or by **P&L per unit of risk**: the mean change of its P&L per unit of time over the standard deviation of those changes. The candidates show best first (the top ten), each with its P&L, lots, edge and markout there, to start (`start_fitted`) or to put in the form.
2. The training period is a tape, as a recording is: either a recording, or a **fresh run** of the selected model from the session as it stands (its book, orders, accounts and strategies, which take part), for a number of events, with random numbers of its own from a seed. The fresh run does not use the session's own generator, so the market a fitted strategy then meets live is another draw, not the one it was fitted on (checked: the training run's events differ from the session's next ones). Every candidate replays the same tape from its start, trading for itself on it; as in a backtest on historical data, the order flow does not react to it.
3. A started candidate keeps its training record (which, of how many, by what, its P&L in training), shown in its row on the leaderboard beside its P&L since it started: out of sample. The best of many candidates on one period is likely to have been lucky there, so its training P&L overstates what to expect; fitting the random trader's seed shows this (its candidates differ only by luck).
4. A fit's results equal running each candidate by hand on the same tape (checked for a fresh run and for a recording). It runs in the event loop, a chunk at a time. Fitting momentum's three settings (27 combinations) over a 10,000-event fresh run takes about 1.3 s; all seven of mean reversion's (30 of its 1,458 combinations) over 50,000 events, the most work a fit does, took 8.8 s. A grid of more than 20,000 combinations is sampled without counting its valid ones.

### P. Is the Order Flow Paying? The Regime and the Null
1. In the dashboard's models, providing liquidity pays without skill, and the leaderboard showed it as skill: a quote joiner, which rests at the best bid and ask and nothing else, made money in all twelve runs of four models (+632 to +10,790 over 30,000 events), and the model's own resting orders earn +0.8 to +1.5 ticks a lot 100 events after they fill (60,000 events, two seeds). The ZI flow sends market orders at a fixed rate whatever the spread, and they carry no information, so whoever rests at the best quote collects what they pay.
2. **The regime line** above the leaderboard says so as it happens: whether the flow pays resting orders (amber when the model's resting orders earn more than 0.1 tick a lot, green within 0.1 of even, red below), the spread against the mid's move per trade, and what the model's resting and market orders, and a quote joiner when one runs, earn a lot. The ratio is Wyart et al.'s (2008): the average spread just before each trade over the root mean square of the mid's change from just before one trade to just before the next (`regime`). They find 1.2 to 1.6 in real small-tick markets (c = 1.58 on 68 Paris stocks in 2002, R² 0.96; 1.32 on 155 NYSE stocks in 2005; 1.17 to 1.53 on index futures), where no strategy, providing or taking, is more than marginally profitable; the argument does not apply directly to large-tick markets, where the tick can hold the spread wider. Here, with no strategies: ZI 3.2, Hawkes 3.0, Mike & Farmer (2008) 3.7, Parlour 2.7 (60,000 events, two seeds), and more with quoting strategies in the book (about 5 in ZI), which add depth at the best and steady the mid. The line's averages weigh the last 2,000 trades or so (checked against plain averages).
3. **Vs null**, on each row, is its markout a lot less that of a strategy with no skill on the same side, at the same time: for taking fills, the model's own market orders (which know nothing: the random trader comes out within 0.2 tick of them in three models, two seeds each); for resting fills, the quote joiner's, while one runs (**Add the null** puts one on the board). The model's own resting orders are no null for resting: those that improved the spread earn +0.1 to +0.2 ticks a lot, those that joined the best +0.6 to +1.5, and those placed behind it, which fill only once the price comes to them, +0.9 to +2.5, while the joiner earns +0.3 to +0.9 (40,000 events, four models, two seeds); queue position is not the reason. Measured so in three models and two seeds each: penny jumping comes out level with the joiner (-0.06 to 0.00), Avellaneda-Stoikov above it (+0.15 to +0.37, on fewer lots), momentum below the market orders (-0.01 to -0.48). Fills that come while no joiner runs are not held against one, and a row's tooltip says over how many of its lots it is.
4. The nulls' state is part of a recording's starting state, so a playback reproduces Vs null too; recordings saved before it still load (their markouts lack which side rested, and their accounts the null's sums).

### Q. Two Books for Pairs Trading (`lob/pairs.py`, `lob/copulas.py`, notebooks 10 and 11)
1. **The model.** Each book's traders place orders around its fair value (`AnchoredZIModel`), so it reprices as the fair value moves; the fair values share a common random walk, and each has a part of its own that order flow moves (impact 0.05 tick a lot) and that, with a half-life, decays back: the gap mean-reverts with that half-life. Without one, the gap wanders and impact is permanent; an `Arbitrageur` (2 ticks, 5 lots) then holds the books together (gap sd about 1.5 to 2.4 ticks instead of 12 to 50). With a half-life of 50 and an own volatility of 0.8, each book stays within about 1.7 ticks of its fair value, and the gap of the mids follows the true gap (correlation 0.94), best 4 time units late. The fair values stop at `FLOOR`, 10 ticks (3Q.12).
2. **What the books show.** Engle-Granger finds the reverting books cointegrated in 8 of 8 seeds, the wandering ones in 0 of 8, and with the arbitrageur in 8 of 8. Least squares underestimates the hedge ratio on short samples (median 0.72 over 4,000 time units, against a true 1; 0.92 over 16,000, 0.98 over 64,000): errors in A's price attenuate it. The half-life from the gap of the mids is about twice the true one at short lags (106 against 50 at lag 1), because the books lag their fair values (checked: the tracking error is persistent, lag-1 autocorrelation 0.85).
3. **The trade.** `PairsTrader` (z-score over 400 decisions, entry ±2, flat at the mean, 5 lots a leg, market orders) makes +454 over 20,000 time units (95% interval +339 to +568, 8 of 8 seeds), after crossing costs of 79% of what it earns before them (1.23 ticks a lot). It pays only with a long window and a wide gap: with a window of 400, at gap sds of 6.8 and 10.2 ticks (4 of 4 seeds each) but not 3.4; with a window of 100, never (it enters three times as often). Beside the arbitrageur it loses (-422 a run, 0 of 4) though it would earn +2,349 before costs: the arbitrageur takes the deviations first.
4. **The figures.** `quant_plots` reads daily weights and returns with linear costs, so the record has one row per decision (a card day is one time unit, 252 a card year): what the trader held after each decision, valued at the mids just before its orders, which makes its P&L before costs exactly the fills' (valued after its orders, it would also charge the trader its own impact). Its cost per unit of the record's turnover is set so it pays, in all, what crossing the spreads cost (the turnover also counts weights drifting with prices). 5. **The Pairs view** (key 4; `app/pairs.py`, `static/js/lob-pairs.js`) runs the same model in the dashboard, as a market of its own beside the Trade view's: its own clock, its speed (1 to 500 units of time a second, about 39 events a unit) and its own event stream. Its settings are the fair values' (whether the gap mean-reverts, the half-life, each book's own and the common volatility, impact, B's beta), an arbitrageur (its threshold and lots) and the seed; Apply starts the books over, running on if they were. A drifting beta and a stressed regime are there too (3Q.6). Its level is 1,000 for A and beta times that for B, and its common volatility 1 by default, against notebook 10's 100 and 0.3: at 0.3 the books' returns over a unit of time correlate 0.12, which is no pair. Up to six traders run in it, of the kinds in `TRADER_KINDS`, each with its own settings. A new one can learn from the chart first: it takes in the chart's mids, one every interval rounded to whole units, as far as it can use them (`learn`), so it trades at once instead of watching for a whole window first.
   - **The chart**: five panes on one time axis, one point a unit of time (the last 3,000): both mids and fair values; the gap from the mids and from the fair values, with the chosen trader's levels and markers where its target changed; its signal against its levels (a z-score, or a copula trader's two flags); its hedge against the true beta; and each trader's P&L. Levels are moved into the chart's terms at each point: the center is where the gap would be with the trader's own deviation at 0, so the gap crosses them where its z-score crosses them, whatever its hedge (a test checks a hedge of 1.2 against a beta of 1.5). A mark sits on the first point taken after the decision.
   - **The chart's controls**, as under the Trade chart: a horizontal zoom in points shown (steppers, a logarithmic slider, presets and a badge), Fit (every point, and every pane centered), Center (back to fitting what is shown, also after dragging a price axis), Now (to the newest point, following it), and a readout at the crosshair, which shows in every pane. Panes and height are kept in this browser.

   - **The chart's time axis** is mostly left to Lightweight Charts, which follows the newest point while that is in view and leaves a range the user dragged back where it is (`rightBarStaysOnScroll` makes a zoom keep the right edge, so a zoom does not stop it following; Fit does start it again). Three things it does not do are in `lob-pairs.js`. It keeps the room to the right of the newest point as points come, so a chart framed for 600 points with one in it would push every new point off to the left: until the frame is full, `shiftVisibleRangeOnNewBar` is off (`frame`, `pointsAdded`). And when the chart drops its oldest points, a view of them moves to the oldest left (`afterDropping`). Not `fixLeftEdge`: a chart pinned at its first point gains a point of its own with each new one, which shook it 18 times a second while it filled. **Setting every line at once empties them first** (`setAll` in `lob-panes.js`). 6. **What the two options are for.** With a constant beta a dynamic hedge has nothing to track, and with a Gaussian common factor a copula has no shape to find. `beta_sd` makes beta an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (`dF_B = beta_t dX + dU_B`, so a change of beta does not move B by itself); `stress_share` gives the common factor a stressed regime. Both are off by default, and with them off a seeded run is unchanged, number for number (checked against the code before the change, over every column, and in a test).
7. **The Kalman trader** (Chan 2013): the filter regresses B's mid on A's with a slope and level that may drift, started by least squares over the first window. `tests/test_pairs.py` holds it to exact least squares with no state noise, the local level's steady gain, and the Gaussian posterior of the whole path. It differs from Chan's in three ways. The regressor is measured from a pivot near the prices (`move_pivot`, which changes the coordinates and not the line). One setting, a memory M, sets the state noise at q = r / M², the filter's time constant. And z is the error over a running average of its size, not the filter's own variance: with a persistent gap that gave z-scores with a standard deviation near 2, so an entry of 2 was really 1.
8. **What notebook 11 finds about it.** The filter behaves like rolling least squares over about twice its memory (root mean square errors of 0.322 and 0.325 with a common volatility of 2). Two things limit any such estimate: A's own noise attenuates it, and the gap, the regression's noise, is persistent, leaving about W / 144 independent observations in a window of W. Those two predict least squares' errors within about a quarter (0.234, 0.119 and 0.060 against 0.247, 0.144 and 0.081). Total least squares does not help: about the same where it is stable, and an error above 100 where it is not. Like for like (the same filter with the slope frozen, the same span, 8 seeds, 17,500 units): with beta fixed, learning the hedge costs money (+655 against +1,055); with beta drifting by 0.3 and a common volatility of 1 there is nothing to win, as even a perfect hedge makes +941 against a fixed hedge's +915 (the filter +545); with beta drifting by 0.5 and a common volatility of 2, costs decide it (+2,036 before costs and +5 after, against +456 and +16), and in books four times as deep the filter pays (+702, 8 of 8 seeds, against +183, 4 of 8, under a ceiling of +1,264), though a z-score with a window of 2,000, at +465 (se 201), is not clearly behind it. Following the hedge while holding adds nothing measurable.
9. **The copula trader** follows Rad, Low and Faff (2016), read in the original: m = h - 0.5 for each book, added up into flags from 0 each trading period; open when one flag is above D and the other below -D; unwind when both have returned to zero (not necessarily at once: this reading is ours). Returns are over 5 units, the window 400 decisions, the period 200, margins empirical. They set D = 0.5 "arbitrarily"; here it trades too much in three of four markets, and the dashboard's default is 1 (a level chosen by looking at these runs). **What notebook 11 finds.** The chain from the market to the family works: Student t in 8 of 8 seeds under stress both ways (AIC 140 below the Gaussian), Clayton in 8 of 8 under falling stress (lower tail 0.35, upper 0), and near-ties with no regimes. Yet the family hardly matters to the P&L: chosen by AIC it adds +62 a run to a Gaussian always (se 54), against +187 to no dependence at all (se 43). The flags drift: with no fresh start their half-life is about 3,300 units against the gap's 73, mostly because a window's margins are off center, so each step has a small mean (with margins centered on the whole run, about 480), and the rest is the ranks' nonlinearity. Within a period they follow the gap's change since the fresh start (correlation 0.92), so they say nothing of where its mean is, which is the likeliest reason a z-score with a window of 2,000 beats it (+1,085 against +407, +1,118 against +327, +721 against +301), the order Rad et al. find (38 against 5 basis points a month after costs). Except in falling stress, where they come level (+248, se 157, against +191 at D = 1 and +300 at D = 2): there, in 10% of the time, the z-score does 44% of its lots, at 6.6 ticks a lot against 1.6 in calm, and the copula 7%, as ranks cap the large moves.
10. **Fitting a trader** (the panel *Fit it over training runs*; `TrainedMarket.fit_trader` in `app/training.py`, which the Stat-arb view shares, 3R.6). The Trade view's fit replays a tape the candidate cannot move. Here a trader's orders move the books, and that is part of what it pays, so each candidate trades alone in fresh markets from the view's settings (`training_run`, `lob.pairs.score_trader`). The seeds (`training_seed`) are above any the seed setting allows, so a training run is never the live market's draw. Every run gets the longest warm-up among the settings tried (`warm_up`), so all are measured over the same span of a market of the same age. `fit_plan` keeps a fit within `MAX_FIT_CANDIDATES` and `MAX_FIT_UNITS` (candidates times runs times time) by a random sample from the seed, and always includes the user's own settings, first if they are not in the grid (`plan_fit`, whose budget is the view's). The default fits take 9 to 17 s here on 15 workers at about 2,500 units a second each; a machine with fewer cores takes proportionally longer. The runs go to a spawned process pool made for the fit (`_fit_pool`, at lower priority, one fit at a time on a server, whichever view, the slot taken only once the pool is made), holding no lock, so the market on the chart runs on untouched; a test checks its random state. Progress goes out in `pairs_state` and the status panel, to every tab, with a Cancel. A Cancel, a failed run or the session's end stops the runs that have begun, with their processes (`_stop_workers`), and a worker ends when the server does, killed or not (`_end_with_server`), so neither leaves cores busy or idle workers behind. The results come by the stream as `pairs_fit`, apart from the panels so that a push never takes their buttons, and also on connect, so a reload brings them back. `rank_candidates` gives each candidate's mean, its standard error, and its difference from the user's settings seed by seed with that difference's standard error; `TrainingFit.verdict` says whether the best beats them by `CLEARLY` (2) standard errors. `start_fitted` starts a candidate, keeping its training result on its row beside its P&L a unit of time here since it had a signal (`trades_from`), with a word if the market's settings have changed since (`same_market`, the seed aside). A live rate over a short time is mostly noise: the row's training standard error is there to remind.
11. **What notebook 11's section 6 finds about fitting** (five fits at the panel's defaults, tested on 16 seeds none of the settings was chosen on, over 17,500 units; P&L from the starting settings, then taking the best in training always, then only when clearly better): the z-score +587, +1,255 and +1,255 with a common volatility of 1, +278, +856 and +856 under stress; the Kalman trader +682, +936 and +784, and +316, +553 and +463; the copula +277, +260 and +272, and +307, +225 and +296. The lengths come from a wider run kept out of the notebook, of 5,000 or 10,000 units with 1, 3 or 5 runs, tested on seeds 1 to 8: three runs of 10,000 did about as well as five of them in three fifths of the time, and better than three of 5,000 (for the Kalman trader +914 against +704, and +381 against +298). A t-test's threshold in place of two standard errors (4.3 at three runs) moved in only 7 of 36 fits, giving up most of the z-score's gain (+922 and +457 against +1,278 and +1,041). **Correcting an earlier assumption:** two traders on a seed share a market only until the first trade, which changes the books, so the rates, so what every later random number is used for. From then on their common factors' changes correlate about zero, and their P&Ls on a seed correlate from about 0 to 0.38, the Kalman trader's most. So comparing by seed takes out little of the luck, here and in notebook 11's section 5; the standard errors are measured from the differences, so they stay right, and about as large as with different seeds. The same care showed the copula's default in a better light than it deserves: chosen on seeds 1 to 8, it was first of 15 there, and fourth with a common volatility of 1 (first under stress, among near equals) on 16 fresh ones.
12. **The floor.** A user who kept the Pairs tab running for a long time saw both prices go negative. The common factor is a random walk in ticks, so from 1,000 at a common volatility of 1 a typical path reaches zero after about a million units of time, roughly half an hour at top speed; the level was never "far from the floor" except over short runs. The fair values went on down (to -160 in a reproduction), while orders stop at one tick (`max(1, ...)` in `AnchoredZIModel`), so each book collapsed to one price and one side: no mid for up to 75% of the time, a meaningless gap, and a trader still paying to cross. So `FairValues` now holds both at or above `floor` (`FLOOR`, 10 ticks: the lowest at which orders up to 8 ticks from it all stay above one tick). What it takes is a push of the common factor at the end of `advance`, just as far as needed, moving A by the push and B by beta times it; `pushed` keeps the total, and the status line shows it once it is above 0. A trade's impact can dip a fair value below the floor by a fraction of a tick until the next event's `advance`, so orders never see it. Pushing the common factor rather than a book leaves the gap alone, in every setting, because the gap nets the common factor out; no random number is drawn, so a seeded run that never reaches the floor is unchanged, number for number (checked at level 60 against level 3,000, and on 40 runs in notebook 11's markets, all identical, whose lowest fair value was 2,143). What it does change is the common factor near the floor, which is no longer a martingale there. Checked in the market that collapsed: no mid missing, and the same gap sd (6.3 to 6.9 against 6.0 to 7.3), spread (1.91 to 1.94 against 1.90 to 1.92) and cost a lot (1.31 to 1.41 against 1.28 to 1.41) as far from it. **Notebook 10 had met the same flaw:** starting from 100, its longer and wandering runs had fair values down to -158, so its stored numbers changed when it was run again: beside the arbitrageur -396 to -422 (and +2,284 to +2,349 before costs), the lone trader in wandering books -1,046 to -1,232 with a window of 400 and -3,354 to -3,347 with 100, and the smallest gap with a window of 400 -196 to -204. Its main result (+454), its figures and its conclusions did not. Those rows against a market started at 3,000, which never reaches the floor, say the floor gives the right answer: -409, -1,196, -3,471 and -196 (standard errors 29 to 110). The Trade view's models have no fair value and floor their orders at one tick, so this does not arise there.

### R. Many Books and Stat-Arb (`lob/statarb.py`, `app/statarb.py`, notebook 12)
1. **The model.** As in notebook 10, each book's traders place orders around its fair value. The fair values share k common factors, random walks, through a table of loadings B, and each has a part of its own, mean-reverting and moved by order flow: F_i = level_i + sum_j B_ij X_j + U_i. A portfolio w with w'B = 0 leaves the factors out, so w'F mean-reverts: the books have n - rank(B) independent relations, and a pair is truly cointegrated only if its two rows of B are parallel. Two tables of twelve books and nine relations each: `sector_loadings(3, 4)`, a market factor and one for each sector, with 18 true pairs of 66; and `mixed_loadings(12, 3, seed=3)`, with no true pair and every relation taking at least four books. Loadings can drift around their start (`loading_sd`, with a half-life of 2,000), and then no fixed relation stays one. The floor is 3Q.12's: a push of the first factor, just as far as needed, which no relation sees and which draws no random number.
2. **A random stream of their own.** 3Q.11 left this neither built nor tested. The factors and the shocks to the own parts come on a grid of time (0.2) from `default_rng([seed, 1])`, and the order flow from `[seed, 2]`, so a seed gives the same factors and shocks whatever trades. It helps, modestly: the correlation across seeds of one relation's P&L under two settings goes from -0.03 to +0.41 with two books and +0.32 with six, and the standard deviation of their difference falls to 0.76 and 0.82 of what different seeds give, worth about one and a half times as many runs. No more, because the flow's random numbers mean different things once a trade has changed the books. The same trader earns the same here as in `lob/pairs.py`, within the noise.
3. **The traders.** `RelationTrader` trades the z-score of each of several portfolios (short above `entry`, long below minus it, flat at `exit`), its lots the size times their sum, rounded (its target: with a rate below 1 or a band it goes toward them, 3S.7). Its relations are fixed or chosen from the last `formation` decisions' mids by `johansen_relations`, `best_baskets` (the largest trace statistics among all baskets of `legs` books), `pairs_by_distance` (Gatev et al. 2006) or `pairs_by_pvalue`, and chosen again every `refit`, when a relation on the same books keeps its state and the rest close. `ResidualTrader` is Avellaneda and Lee (2010), read in the original: PCA on the correlation matrix of the moves, each eigenvector over the books' volatilities; each book regressed on those portfolios over a window; the residuals summed and fitted by a lag-1 regression; s = (X - m) / sigma_eq, with m centered across the books; a fit rejected unless b is below exp(-2 / window), their limit of half the window; open beyond 1.25, close a short below 0.75 and a long above -0.5. Hedges stay as they were on entry. `CopulaPortfolio` runs `lob.pairs.CopulaTrader` on each of many pairs chosen by distance, as Rad et al. do for all their methods, with a book allowed in several. `score_portfolio` is `lob.pairs.score_trader` with what the trader held and how much of the factors.
4. **What the tests see** (twelve runs of each market, seeds 101 to 112). Johansen finds almost none of the nine relations over 2,000 units (0.2 to 0.3), 6.2 to 6.7 over 5,000, and all nine over 10,000, which is why traders choose from 10,000. Engle-Granger over 20,000 calls every true pair cointegrated, but it cannot tell one from a pair that nearly is: in the mixed market, where no pair is, it calls 91% of those with under 0.3 of a factor left, 46% with 0.3 to 0.7 and 8% with more; a unit-root test is known to reject too often when a small random walk sits beside a large stationary part (Schwert 1989). Across sectors its 10% comes in bunches: in 4 of 12 runs about 14 of the 48 pairs at once.

6. **The Stat-arb view** (key 5; `app/statarb.py`, `static/js/lob-statarb.js`). A market of its own beside the Pairs view's, with its own clock, speed (up to 500 units of time a second) and event stream; `app/live.py` and `lob-panes.js` hold what the two share. Its settings choose sectors or mixed loadings and how many, the volatilities, half-life, impact, seed, and drifting loadings; Apply starts the books over, and is refused, with the reason, if a residual trader on the board could not trade in the new market. Up to six traders of eight kinds (`TRADER_KINDS`): pairs, baskets, residuals and copula pairs, whose defaults are those that did best in section 4, and the four that score every book (3S.5). A new one can learn from the mids kept (20,000 units, more than the chart's 3,000), so it need not watch for a whole formation first.
   - **The chart:** four panes on one time axis, with the Pairs chart's controls: the books' mids, colored by sector; the signal of the trader shown, one line a relation, against its levels; what that trader holds, in lots and of the factors; and each trader's P&L.

   - **Choosing runs elsewhere** (`_choose_elsewhere`, one thread), so the market goes on meanwhile and the trader takes up the answer at its first decision after that. That is so for a copula trader too, whose pairs and copulas (`CopulaPortfolio._build`) are the work, and which then learns the mids that came meanwhile. With the defaults, distance and baskets take under a second at any speed. P-values take 3.7 s at the default speed and 11.6 s at top speed, when the market's thread has most of the interpreter, while the market goes on 5,751 units. A market stepped flat out, as in a test, can starve it for much longer, so the tests wait for the answer rather than for market time.
   - **The fit panel** is the Pairs view's (`fit_panel` in `panes.html`, `lobFitPanel` in `lob-panes.js`, `app/training.py`), over the books as they are now, on seeds the live market never has; 3R.7 says what it finds. A default fit takes about a minute on 15 workers in the twelve sectors books (61 s for pairs, 65 for baskets and 69 for copula pairs, measured; 40 for residuals, whose warm-up is shorter), and only one runs at a time on a server, whichever view. ### S. More Long/Short Strategies (`lob/crosssection.py`, notebook 13)
1. **A book of longs and shorts.** Notebook 12's traders each bet that a gap closes. These hold lots in every book at once, in proportion to a score of each. At a rebalance a `CrossSectionTrader` takes the scores less their projection on a row of ones and on the books' exposures to `factors` estimated factors (`neutralize`, by least squares), and holds `size` lots of the book with the largest weight and the rest in proportion, rounded to whole lots (halves to the even). The weights sum to 0 and have no exposure to any factor; the lots, rounded and built on estimates, keep a little of each: for the new traders in notebook 13's section 3, 1.6 to 4.6 lots of exposure to the true factors (root mean square) against about 25 lots held. `estimate_factors` is the PCA of `ResidualTrader` (the correlation matrix of the standardized moves, each eigenvector over the books' standard deviations; the exposures from regressing each book's demeaned moves on the eigenportfolios'), made again every `pca_every` (20) decisions from the last `pca_window` (500) moves. Equal scores leave nothing to hold, and a trader that holds something closes it. It rebalances every `hold` decisions and sits between (unless it trades less, 3S.7); `entries` counts books newly held long or short. It needs more books than its `factors` and one (`attach` refuses fewer), and holds nothing until it has the mids its score or its factors need (`needed`; `warm_up` is that many decisions and one more, in time). `learn` takes in past mids.
2. **Four scores.** `ReversalTrader` buys the books that fell over the last `lookback` decisions and sells those that rose, after Lehmann (1990) and Lo and MacKinlay (1990): a book's score is minus the sum of its residual moves (its moves less what the factors take of them; with no factors, its moves). `MomentumTrader` is Jegadeesh and Titman (1993): the same sum, plus, over `lookback` decisions that ended `skip` decisions ago (with a skip of 0, reversal turned over). `LeadLagTrader` (Lo and MacKinlay 1990; Hou 2007) regresses each book's move on all the books' moves one decision before, by ridge regression over the last `pca_window` moves, demeaned (the penalty is `ridge` times the regressors' average variance; a book's own lag is left out, since that is reversal, so the matrix `gamma` has a zero diagonal), and scores a book by its row of `gamma` times the last decision's moves. `FlowTrader` buys the books with the buying in their order flow, after Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov (2014), as `measure` says: `"trades"`, the change in a book's net bought lots over the last `lookback` decisions over the change in its volume (the share of what traded that was bought; 0 with no volume), or `"queues"`, the lots at the best bid less those at the best ask, over their sum, now (0 with a side empty). `contrarian_decomposition` is Lo and MacKinlay's split of a contrarian portfolio's profit at a lag into the books' own autocovariances, their cross-autocovariances and the dispersion of their means; the parts add to the profit.
3. **Seeing the flow.** `BookMarket.flow` and `volume` hold, a book each, the lots of the models' own orders that traded on arrival: `flow` signed (plus when the order bought), `volume` not. 8. **Sparse baskets (stage 3).** Notebook 12's baskets come from Johansen's trace test (`best_baskets` tests every combination of a few books; `johansen_relations` takes the vectors on all of them). Stage 3 asks whether choosing them another way trades better: by Box and Tiao's (1977) *predictability*. Fit a VAR(1) to the books' levels; a portfolio's predictability is the share of its variance that the books' lagged levels explain, about 1 for a random walk and near 0 for noise, so the least predictable portfolio is the most mean-reverting. Over all the books it has a leg in each; asking for few legs is d'Aspremont's (2011) NP-hard sparse problem, which he attacks with a greedy search and `lob/sparse_baskets.py` follows. Notebook 13's section 6 asks what each chooser picks in the two markets, and how the baskets trade with section 5's rate.
   - **The library** (`lob/sparse_baskets.py`; it draws no random number). `fit_var1(levels, ridge=0)` fits x_t = A x_{t-1} + e_t by least squares to the levels less their means and gives `A` and `gamma`, the covariance of the lagged levels (X0'X0 over T - 1; `ridge` is a share of the average variance added to the diagonal, and a singular fit gets the minimum-norm answer); it needs `n + 2` rows, since at `n + 1` the fit is exact. Box and Tiao's `gamma` is the covariance of the levels under stationarity, which is the same; d'Aspremont's sample version divides by the current levels' covariance, and on eight real runs the two least eigenvalues agree to within 0.002 on 2,000 rows (0.01 on 400). `predictability(w, a, gamma)` is w'A gamma A'w / w'gamma w, which does not depend on the scale of `w`. `box_tiao(a, gamma, books=None)` solves the generalized symmetric eigenproblem M w = lambda gamma w, with M = A gamma A', on the books given (all if none), and gives the eigenvalues, the predictabilities, least first, and their rows of weights (zero outside the books). `greedy_basket(a, gamma, start, legs)` grows a set of books from `start` one at a time, each time adding the book that gives the least smallest eigenvalue (ties to the lowest index), and gives `(lambda, books, row)`. `sparse_baskets(history, count=1, legs=3, every=5, ridge=0)` grows a basket from every pair of books (at `legs` 2 the pairs themselves), keeps each distinct set once, ranks the sets by least predictability, and gives `count` rows of weights with the largest leg 1, the shape of `best_baskets`, so a `RelationTrader` takes it as its `choose`; as in `best_baskets`, the baskets that share no book with a better one come first. With `legs` 0 it gives the least predictable portfolios of all the books (`box_tiao`), dense, like `johansen_relations`, at most as many as there are books.
   - **What it leaves out**, as its docstrings say. Any test of whether a basket is a relation: with `legs` of 2 or more it gives the `count` baskets asked for (fewer only when the distinct sets run out) whether or not any is one, where Johansen's choosers give only what the trace test accepts, and a book added for a small gain in sample can bring a factor in, as a third book from another sector does in a market of sectors; with `legs` 0, the rows past the number of true relations are not relations. A floor on a basket's swings: predictability is scale-free, so it does not see a swing against what the legs cost to trade, and Cuturi and d'Aspremont (2013) add a variance threshold so that a basket is not chosen for swinging too little to pay for its legs. d'Aspremont's other searches, a semidefinite relaxation and an exact one: the greedy search is the cheapest and can be far from the least predictable set of its size, as he notes, since the best sets of different sizes need not nest. And lags beyond the first. It also rewards unpredictable noise in the mids as much as mean reversion of the fair values, which is dormant here, where every book's order flow is alike and a mid's lag behind its fair value cancels within a relation.

## 7. Known Limitations

- Sessions are held in memory: lost on restart, separate per server process, capped at 50 (least recently seen evicted) and evicted after 30 minutes idle without an open tab.
- The simplified Mike-Farmer model concentrates depth at the best quote and its book is too thin to measure impact; use the 2008-structure model for those questions.
- Two verdicts in notebook 05 stay open because their values sit next to the bar. Mike-Farmer (2008 structure) volatility clustering is 0.099 against a bar of 0.1, so more seeds will not settle it. Parlour's excess kurtosis is 0.95 against a bar of 1; settling that would take about 915 seeds, 29 times the 32 used. The notebook explains both.
- The stylized-facts panel measures one run: its standard errors are approximate (too small under long memory), and with the dashboard's default rates the Hawkes model's clustering sits on the bar. Use notebook 05's values and 2M events to compare with the notebook.
- The Mike & Farmer (2008) estimators are checked on simulated data only, also in LOBSTER's format: the repository has no order-level market data (LOBSTER's free samples come through a request form). Notebook 08 fits any LOBSTER files put in `data/lobster/` (ignored by git); real data also have varying sizes, hidden orders, partial cancellations and intraday seasonality, which the model lacks.
- The order book's levels view lists only levels with orders; the tick view covers 40 ticks either side of the mid. One-click orders are plain limit orders (no iceberg peak).
- No model reproduces the square-root law of metaorder impact: impact grows about linearly (faster in Mike & Farmer's) and does not revert (notebook 09). Their order flow does not react to a metaorder.
- Recordings live in the session's memory (three at once, 500,000 events each) until downloaded. In a playback the recorded traders do not react to the user's or the strategies' orders; split orders replay as their slices; replayed strategies' signals are recomputed by the current code while their orders come from the tape; Exit playback starts the model afresh on the book.
- The models' order flow pays liquidity providers without skill (3P): a resting strategy's P&L is mostly the flow's, as Vs null and the regime line show. The models' orders carry no information about future prices, as real ones can; replaying real LOBSTER days, a queue-reactive model (Huang, Lehalle and Rosenbaum 2015) or latency would make a fairer test.
- Notebook 12's books all have the same volatility and half-life, so all relations revert at the same speed and Johansen's vectors are arbitrary within their space. Its traders are alone, with market orders only, and twelve books are few for stat-arb: what breadth and netting do at a thousand names once a day is not tested. Its defaults are what did best on notebook 12's seeds 1 to 8, in its two markets, and the grids the Stat-arb fit searches were set on the same seeds, so where a default is first of its grid the fit has little to find; it tries them on seeds the live market never has, and three fits to a row cannot say how often *clearly better* is right (3R.7).
- Notebook 13 (3S) has one type of market, twelve books with two tables of loadings, and its traders use market orders only, each alone; passive orders could change every comparison that costs decide, and are where a signal right for a few units of time might pay. Its settings come from grids on seeds 1 to 8: small ones in sections 3 and 4 (up to three a kind, one for momentum), and larger ones, which the notebook does not print, for section 5's test, whose frontier shows the best of three to five points along a curve on the eight seeds that report it. So the best of a kind or a curve is the best of a few, and its counts of settings above two standard errors are over a list fixed in advance, not tests. At high turnover the before-cost figure holds a trader's own impact on the mid it is marked at, so it is not the worth of a signal (section 2's listeners measure that without trading). The order flow is known exactly from the model, which says whether each order bought or sold, where a real trader infers the aggressor from quotes and prints, with errors; the counters hold only the models' orders that traded on arrival, not the traders' own, nor cancellations or resting orders.
- Notebook 13's section 5 (3S.7) has a frontier in one market, the sectors, on eight seeds, and a test of four families and not seven (momentum, which loses in section 3, is not in it). Its aim is not Gârleanu and Pedersen's: it is the trader's own rule's, scaled to its size and not scaled down by the signal's decay or the costs, so the rate and the band show what going part of the way and a band do to these traders, and not what their costs make best. Most of its paired differences compare a family's bests, chosen on seeds 1 to 8, and not one setting changed, and the best of a curve is the best of three to five points on the seeds that report it. The residuals' best rate (entering beyond 0.5, at 0.1) and the queues' (0.02) were at the edge of what was tried on seeds 1 to 8, so a lower one might do better. A band roughly doubles the residuals' exposure to the factors, from 1.6 and 1.9 lots in section 3's rows to 3.8 and 3.5 in the sectors and mixed markets, and the residuals' card with a band holds 1.10 times its capital at its most (the capital of 150,000 is meant to be more than a card holds at its most). A band applies to each book alone, so one leg of a relation can sit inside it while another trades; with a rate or a band `entries` count the states asked for, not the trades. The Pairs view's traders (`lob/pairs.py`) have neither setting.
- Notebook 13's section 6 (3S.8) compares two choosers of baskets of three and four books from twelve (and Box-Tiao's of all twelve, and pairs) in the same two markets. Box-Tiao's chooser tests nothing: it gives the baskets asked for whether or not any is a relation, where Johansen's gives only those its trace test accepts. Predictability is scale-free and does not see a basket's swing against what its legs cost to trade (the least predictable chooser, the baskets of all twelve books, swings least per lot), and the variance threshold of Cuturi and d'Aspremont (2013) is not added; it also rewards unpredictable noise in the mids as much as mean reversion of the fair values, which is dormant here. The search is greedy, from every pair, and not exhaustive, so it can miss the least predictable set of its size, and it fits one lag. Each chooser's entry and rate were chosen on seeds 1 to 8 from a small grid (four entries and three rates, which the notebook does not print), except the dashboard's default baskets row and Box-Tiao's 4 of 4 at an entry of 1, so a chooser's own best is not like for like with another's. Johansen's chooser can hand the trader a basket with no relation in it (it takes first the baskets that share no book with a better one, so its fourth of four baskets of 3 from twelve books can be a triple apart from the first three, taken whenever its trace statistic passes the 95% value); the picks show none of its baskets of 3 leaving more than 0.050 in the sectors market and 0.548 at most in the mixed, and the notebook does not show how often a trader on other seeds holds one or what it does to its P&L, so that the two choosers earn about the same at the same settings holds for the seeds drawn, and may not for others. What the choosers pick is from one history a seed, the one a trader passes to its chooser at its first choice (the traded baskets, checked by an assert on seed 101 for Box-Tiao's 4 of 3), on eight of the twelve seeds the tables use; the seconds to choose are the laptop's, medians of one call on one core while other seeds' choices ran, to compare the choosers with each other. The Stat-arb fit's grids for the rate and the band of baskets (3S.7) were not set from section 6, and a fit keeps the choice of chooser and tries the numbers: that a fit of Box-Tiao baskets works is tested, and not whether it helps.
- The Stat-arb view's four cross-sectional kinds are tested for how they work, and not for whether fitting them helps: notebook 13 does not fit them, and only reversal pays (its default is now section 5's best, with a rate; order flow and lead-lag still lose at every setting tried). What a fit proposes for the rate and the band of every kind (3S.7) was set from notebook 13's section 5 on seeds 1 to 8, which has no baskets, copula pairs or momentum, and no fit over those values has been tested for any kind. A flow trader has no past flow or queues to learn from and waits to see them once it starts. Lead-lag's default (hold 20, every 1) is the setting of its kind that loses most.
- Fitting a trader on the Pairs or Stat-arb view runs each candidate alone, where on the chart it trades among whatever else is there, and compares them on seeds they share only until the first trade (3Q.11). *Clearly better* does not allow for having picked the best of many, and each panel says so: with nine settings truly equal it says *clearly better* in 27.8% of fits (3R.7).
- Fitting replays its training period without the market reacting to the candidate, as a backtest on historical data does; a fresh training run starts the model's own state over (the Hawkes intensity, the long-memory signs) on the session's book. A fit runs one at a time per session, without a way to stop it once started.
- Strategies decide only as model events arrive, at their interval (so not in the manual model), and in these models nobody trades on information, so stale quotes cost little; P&L is in ticks times lots, without fees. They also trade during a facts collection, which then measures the market with them in it. A strategy sees only the best quotes and the lots at them.
- The heatmap covers 30 ticks either side of the mid and the last 600 units of simulation time, and starts over after a reset or a collection.
- The notebooks filter joblib's "worker stopped" warning: its workers restart when their memory has grown 300 MB since their first task, which happens after a few large simulations. No work is lost.

## 8. Recommendations & Future Enhancements

1. **Calibration on market data**: request LOBSTER's free samples, put them in `data/lobster/` and run notebook 08's last cell (`calibrate_lobster`, with a burn-in of at least its `last_unknown`); then offer the result as a parameter set in the dashboard.
2. **Stat-arb**: choose the number of PCA factors by a share of the variance, as Avellaneda and Lee also do (notebook 12 knows the true number); give books different half-lives and volatilities; try passive orders where costs decide the comparison; a verdict that allows for having picked the best of many; and more fits to a row in notebook 12's section 10 (it has three), with finer entries near 1.25 to 1.5 for pairs and baskets, to say how often *clearly better* is right.
3. **Concave metaorder impact**: add latent liquidity (orders held back and revealed as the price approaches, as in Tóth et al. 2011) to a model and test it with notebook 09's design.
4. **More long/short strategies**: the last stage of notebook 13 (3S.6): an index book, deal events and a persistent factor. Trading less (3S.7) and sparse baskets (3S.8) are built; fitting the rate and the band over training runs, as notebook 12's section 10 does for the other settings, is not tested, nor is a fit over the two choosers of baskets. What stage 3 leaves out (3S.8): a variance threshold on Box-Tiao's baskets and a test of whether one is a relation.
